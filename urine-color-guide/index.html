Urine Color Hydration Guide

A quick, low-tech hydration check based on the study-validated Armstrong 8-color scale — plus the situations where urine color should not be trusted at all.

Check your color

Compare your urine in a clear glass held against a white background in good light — not in the toilet bowl, where the water dilutes and distorts the color. Keep in mind that first-morning urine is usually darker than the rest of the day.

First — are you 65 or older?

For adults aged 65 and over, urine color must not be used to assess hydration. ESPEN geriatrics guidance gives this its highest evidence grade — age-related kidney changes make color unreliable. Please use the Senior Hydration tool instead.

About the Armstrong scale

Where it comes from

8-color urine chart developed by Dr. Lawrence Armstrong; validated in field and lab studies as a practical hydration marker for healthy adults.

How to read it

Levels 1–3: hydrated
Levels 4–6: mildly dehydrated
Levels 7–8: dehydrated

Studies show urine color tracks hydration status reasonably well in healthy younger adults, which is why sports and military medicine have used the Armstrong chart for decades. As a practical guide, it works best as a trend over several days rather than a single reading. It is not reliable in adults 65 and over — ESPEN geriatrics guidance assigns that caution its highest evidence grade — nor when foods, vitamins or medications tint the urine.

Disclaimer

This guide is educational only and is not medical advice. Urine color is a rough screening clue, not a diagnosis. If you have symptoms that worry you, or a condition affecting your kidneys or fluid balance, consult a healthcare professional.
