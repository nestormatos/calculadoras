This estimate covers everything you drink during the flight, including moisture from food — it is not extra on top of your daily goal. Alcohol and excess caffeine during the flight make dehydration worse, so go easy on both. On long flights, concentrate your intake while you are awake so your sleep is not interrupted.

Drinking well does not prevent altitude sickness. If you develop a severe headache, confusion, shortness of breath at rest, or clumsy walking, descend and seek medical care. Thirst decreases at altitude, so drink on a schedule — without forcing large amounts at once.

Also useful: the Hot Weather Water Calculator for destination climates, or the Sweat Rate Calculator if you will be hiking at altitude.

How the estimates are built

Flight estimate

During flight: 3.4–10.1 fl oz per hour
Highlighted midpoint: hours × 6.8 fl oz

Altitude adjustment

Below 1,500 m: +0 fl oz
1,500–2,500 m: +0 to 17 fl oz
2,500 m and above: +34 to 51 fl oz

The in-flight range reflects commonly cited aerospace-medicine guidance: studies show cabin humidity at cruise is only about 10–20%, which raises insensible fluid losses. The altitude tiers are conservative design thresholds — there is no official source that specifies exact extra volumes — so treat them as a practical guide, not a medical target. Disclaimer

General guidance for healthy people — not medical advice. If you have kidney, heart or liver disease, or you have been told to restrict fluids or sodium, follow your doctor's instructions. Drinking too much can also be dangerous: it can lower blood sodium (hyponatremia). Every number on this page is an estimate based on limited evidence; if you are travelling to very high altitude, talk to a healthcare professional first.
