How the calculation works

The Precision Hydration Engine applies the widely used weight-based guideline for daily water intake. Your result is a practical baseline for healthy adults, not a medical prescription.

Imperial (pounds)

Daily target (fl oz) = body weight (lb) × 0.50

Metric (kilograms)

Daily target (fl oz) = body weight (kg) × 1.10
  • 1 US fl oz is the base unit for every conversion on this page.
  • Standard bottle equivalents: 16.9 fl oz and 20 fl oz.
  • Both factors are equivalent: since 1 kg ≈ 2.205 lb, kg × 1.10 gives the same result as lb × 0.50.

Frequently asked questions

Straight answers about weight-based hydration, adjusted needs and safe limits.

How much water should I drink for my weight?

A widely used baseline is half your body weight in pounds, expressed in fluid ounces per day — equivalently, kilograms multiplied by 1.10. For example, a 154 lb (70 kg) adult would target about 77 fl oz, roughly 2.3 liters per day.

Treat this as a starting point and adjust for activity, climate and your own thirst.

Is this the same as the “8 glasses a day” rule?

The classic 8×8 rule (eight 8-ounce glasses, or 64 fl oz) is easy to remember but ignores body size. A weight-based target scales with your body, so it is generally more personalized — lighter people need less, heavier people need more.

When should I drink more than my calculated target?

Increase your intake during exercise (an extra 12–24 fl oz around workouts), in hot or humid weather, at high altitude, and during fever, vomiting or diarrhea. Pregnancy and breastfeeding also raise fluid needs — follow your healthcare provider’s guidance in those cases.

Can I drink too much water?

Yes. Drinking excessive amounts in a short period can dilute blood sodium (hyponatremia), which can be dangerous. Spread your intake across the day, drink to thirst, and include electrolytes during prolonged endurance exercise.

Do coffee, tea and food count toward my daily target?

Yes. All beverages and water-rich foods (fruit, vegetables, soups) contribute to your total water intake. Caffeinated drinks have a mild diuretic effect, but for most people they still provide a net positive contribution.

Medical disclaimer

This tool provides general educational estimates for healthy adults only. Results are not medical advice and should not replace guidance from a qualified healthcare professional.

If you are pregnant or breastfeeding, or live with kidney disease, heart failure, diabetes or any other condition that affects fluid balance — or if you take medication that influences hydration — consult your doctor before changing how much you drink.
